Karaoke Machine

A five-block audio circuit with tone control, karaoke voice suppression and LED volume feedback, taken from hand calculations to a soldered PCB.

Course
EE 210, Fall 2024
Role
Design, simulation and build
Tools
Multisim, breadboard, oscilloscope, soldering
Blocks
Mixer, tone, volume, LED display, attenuator

Overview

The Tone Control and Karaoke Circuit takes any audio input through a 3.5 mm jack and passes it through five soldered blocks: a mixer/karaoke stage, tone control, volume control, an LED volume display and an attenuator. It lets you control bass, treble and volume, or suppress the vocals on a track.

Class project: EE 210, Fall 2024. Each block was designed by hand, simulated in Multisim, built on a breadboard and tested with an oscilloscope before the final PCB.

Block by block

For each block: the design goal, how it works, and the evidence at each stage. Click any figure to enlarge.

1

Mixer / karaoke

Goal. Process the audio from the 3.5 mm jack, which has a left prong, a right prong and a ground. An SPDT switch chooses between mixing the channels, Vout = −(VL + VR), and suppressing the vocals, Vout = VL − VR, which removes components that are equally distributed across both channels.

How it works. An op-amp circuit that works as an inverting summing amplifier in mixer mode and a subtracting amplifier in karaoke mode. Matched resistors give a gain of one, and 1% tolerance parts matter most in karaoke mode so the subtraction is precise.

2

Tone control

Goal. Modify the signal's frequency content by independently adjusting bass and treble.

How it works. A Baxandall tone-control circuit with two potentiometers, one for bass and one for treble. The resistors set the gain range, designed to run from 1/10 to 10, so the signal can drop to 10% of its amplitude or be amplified ten times.

3

Volume control

Goal. Adjust the signal amplitude to control the output volume before the later blocks.

How it works. A single potentiometer varies the voltage at its output terminal as it is turned.

4

LED volume display

Goal. Give a visual indication of the audio amplitude with four LEDs at different voltage levels.

How it works. A comparator network. Resistor values set a reference voltage for each LED, at 1.5 V, 1 V, 0.5 V and 0.25 V. When the signal exceeds a reference, the next LED lights, so more LEDs light as the volume rises. Limiting resistors protect the LEDs.

5

Attenuator

Goal. Reduce the signal so it sits at a comfortable level for headphones.

How it works. An inverting op-amp whose resistors set a gain below one. The output is meant to stay between 0.5 V and 1 V. Scaling the scope channels showed a gain of 1/20, as designed.

Build process

I first drew the schematic on paper and did every calculation to set the resistor values and connections. To find mistakes early, I built each block on its own and put an oscilloscope on its input and output to confirm it did its job.

For the PCB, I soldered in a specific order to make it easier: resistors, IC sockets, capacitors, audio jacks, the switch, the LEDs, and finally the potentiometers. I tested it using the audio equalizer of the myDAQ to protect the board.

What went wrong, and how I caught it

  • In block 4, I first placed the limiting resistors after the LEDs instead of before them. I corrected this in the Multisim and breadboard designs.
  • In block 1, the switch did not change between mixer and karaoke output correctly. Testing the block alone showed a resistor was not connected to the breadboard terminals properly.
