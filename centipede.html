Centipede

Measuring the real energy cost of a centipede robot's motors, from a load cell on the bench to a current sensor on the robot.

The goal: a trustworthy cost of energy

To compare gaits and terrains, I need to know how much mechanical work the robot's motors really do. The plan has four steps.

Baseline each motor

Run the exact motion profile unloaded, with the joint decoupled, and log torque τ0(t). Repeat for every motor, because friction varies from unit to unit.

Log real trials

Run the same profile with the joint connected. Log torque τ(t) and encoder position θ(t) for all motors at a fixed sample rate.

Compute work per joint

Subtract the baseline to get the load torque, then sum torque times angle change over time and over all motors.

\[ \tau_{load} = \tau - \tau_0 \qquad W = \sum_{t} \tau_{load}(t)\,\Delta\theta(t) \]

Normalize to cost of energy

Divide total work by robot weight times distance travelled, tracked separately, and compare across gaits and terrains.

\[ \text{cost of energy} = \frac{W_{total}}{\text{weight} \times \text{distance}} \]

Validated on the first test (Sep 22).

  • The test runs on a Dynamixel motor and across multiple motors.
  • Baseline subtraction and the work formula execute correctly against real load and position.

Still needs proof.

  • Per-motor baseline calibration, or one shared calibration across motors.
  • Whether idle and holding torque should count toward the cost.

Mechanical work of one lift

A 199.5 g weight raised through half a turn on a 182 mm arm, repeated at four speeds, with and without the weight.

The experiment

Eight sweeps: four speeds (1, 2, 2.5 and 5 rpm), each with and without the weight. One run per condition.

  1. Torque on, tare with no weight
  2. Calibrate with the 56.1 g mass
  3. Sweep the arm from 180° to 359° with Move + Record CSV
  4. Repeat at 1, 2, 2.5 and 5 rpm, with and without 199.5 g
  5. Run analyzeMotorWork on all eight CSV files

The motor is sampled about 6 times per second and the load cell about 10 times per second.

The experiment in motion

What it shows. With no load, torque and work are near zero. With the weight, torque peaks near 0.37 N·m and the net work is about 0.70 J, and both are essentially the same at every speed.

Back-of-envelope check. Holding 0.1995 kg at the end of a 0.182 m arm takes m·g·r ≈ 0.356 N·m, close to the measured 0.37 N·m peak. Lifting through half a turn raises the weight by 2r, so 2·m·g·r ≈ 0.71 J, close to the measured 0.70 J.

Cycle runs on three motors

One lift is a start. Running the arm back and forth for many cycles shows how the torque behaves over time.

I ran 110-cycle tests on three motors (1A, 1B and 2A) and built a MATLAB replay tool to play each run back. The load cell is the reference (gray). Against it sit the Dynamixel's own torque reading (blue), that reading filtered (orange), and the filtered reading scaled by a correction factor k (green).

On the Motor 2A run the motor's raw reading overshoots the load cell on the main peak, about 0.65 against about 0.4 N·m. With k = 0.7 the corrected trace lands closer, and RMSE against the load cell drops from 0.179 to 0.156 N·m.

Tracking one torque peak per cycle also shows the peaks drifting upward over a run, from roughly 0.62 to 0.67 N·m across about 1,400 seconds.

The next goal is a simple model, τ = μ·τd, where τd is the Dynamixel's reported torque and μ is a correction factor that can be fitted per motor and applied to the others.

Reading torque from current

The motor reports a load percentage, but an INA260 power sensor shows the current it really draws.

A linear current model

Fitting the up-stroke gives a straight line from load percentage to current:

\[ I \approx 0.034\ \text{A} + 0.00467\ \text{A} \times \text{load}\,\% \]

Going down, the current stays at about 50 mA while the load reads near 0, so the two directions need to be handled separately.

The sensor sits in the motor's 12 V supply line and is read by an Arduino Uno, so the current can be logged alongside the load cell.
