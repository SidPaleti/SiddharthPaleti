Simulated Warehouse Robot

Can a robot find its way using only its wheels? I built a time-based controller and a sensor-based controller for a simulated differential-drive robot, then tested both with and without noise.

Course ME 456 Task Packing station p2 to item location i1 Software MATLAB, Simulink, Stateflow Toolboxes Robotics Playground, Simscape Multibody
The task Time-based controller Pose estimation Sensor-based controller Adding noise Comparison

The task

The project is a simulation of a differential-drive robot moving between a warehouse packing station and the shelves where items are picked up. The robot has wheels of radius 0.08 m, a wheel spacing of 0.4 m, and two encoders that each count ticks of 1°. The motors take a control signal, and signals of about 30 to 70 work well.

I developed two controllers and compared them. The time-based controller only sends commands to the motors based on how much time has passed, and does not use the encoders. The sensor-based controller estimates the wheel velocities from the encoders, estimates the pose of the robot from the vehicle kinematic model, and uses that pose to choose the control signals. The environment provides the actual pose, but it is only used for plotting and evaluation, never inside the controller.

Both controllers were first developed with the actuators and sensors perfect, then tested with noise on the motors and the encoders. Every run goes from packing station p2 to item location i1.

  • Wheel spacing 0.4 m
  • Encoder tick = 1°
  • Motor signal 30 to 70
  • Route: p2 to i1

Time-based controller

The robot starts at (−2.75, 1.0) m, touching the right edge of p2, and needs to reach i1 at (−0.75, 1.0) m. Both are at y = 1 m, but a straight drive east would run into the first shelf. The route leaves p2 into the vertical aisle, drops to the middle of the main horizontal aisle, drives east to x ≈ −0.75 m and turns north into i1. That makes four straight segments and three 90° turns.

The controller is a Stateflow chart with eight states. Each state sets the left and right motor signals when it is entered, and an after(t, sec) transition passes control to the next state once the segment time has elapsed. Every drive and turn uses a signal of 70: both wheels at +70 drive straight, and +70 on one wheel with −70 on the other turns the robot in place.

The segment times came from calibration runs with noise off. At a signal of 70 the robot moves at about 0.17 m/s and turns at about 29°/s, so a 90° turn takes roughly 3.1 s. Those first values were refined with a script that simulated the route, read the position and heading at the end of each segment, adjusted that segment's time and repeated.

With noise off, the robot ends at (−0.711, 0.998) m with a heading of 92.1°. Its center is inside the i1 box and 0.04 m from the center of the box. It enters the box about 29.4 s into the run and stays there.

Pose estimation

The estimator uses the two encoders and the differential-drive kinematic model. Each encoder signal goes through a Discrete Derivative block (0.1 s sample time) and a gain of 2π/360, which gives the wheel angular velocities ωL and ωR in rad/s. The forward speed and turn rate are then

v = r(ωR + ωL)/2,   ω = r(ωR − ωL)/L

and the pose follows from integrating them: θ = ∫ω dt, x = ∫v cos θ dt and y = ∫v sin θ dt.

The first version used continuous-time integrators, and each derivative sample was applied one step late. The largest heading error on the test motion was 2.48°. The final version uses discrete-time integrators with backward Euler at the 0.1 s sample time, and x and y use the heading at the middle of each step. On the same motion the largest heading error fell to 1.40°.

Testing it

The test runs in the simple environment, a 10 m square, with the robot starting at (0, 0) at 0°. To get motion in x, y and rotation at the same time, a Stateflow chart like the one in the time-based controller drives it through straight drives, an arc to the left, an in-place left turn, an arc to the right and a final straight drive. Over the 46 s run the path is 6.57 m long.

The largest errors are 0.044 m in x, 0.027 m in y and 1.40° in heading, and the root-mean-square errors are 0.019 m, 0.015 m and 0.79°. At the end of the run the position error is 0.050 m over a path of 6.57 m, under 1% of the distance traveled. The x error is largest at the end, which is what dead reckoning is expected to do, since each small reading error stays in the integral.

Sensor-based controller

The sensor-based controller takes the same trip as the time-based one and uses only the encoders. The estimator turns the encoder counts into an estimated pose, and a Stateflow chart uses that estimate to drive the robot through four waypoints: (−2.5, 1.0), (−2.5, 0), (−0.7, 0) and (−0.7, 1.0) m.

It was developed in four steps, each tested before the next was added. The estimator came first, because the controller can only be as good as the pose it reads. Next the route was reduced to four waypoints in the aisles. A Stateflow chart was then written to visit them in order, and finally the gains were adjusted by running the model with noise off.

The chart has four states. In Turn, the robot rotates in place toward waypoint k with a signal proportional to the heading error, limited to between 25 and 60, and moves on when the error is below 0.03 rad. In Drive, the forward signal is proportional to the distance remaining along the leg, limited to between 30 and 70, with a steering term added to one wheel and subtracted from the other to stay on the line to the waypoint. After the last waypoint it enters Done, which sets both signals to zero, and two seconds later Finish ends the simulation.

Two details are worth explaining. The stop test uses the distance remaining along the leg, projected on the heading of the leg, not the straight-line distance. It reaches zero even if the robot has drifted sideways, and goes negative if it overshoots, so a leg always ends. The steering term is also switched off within 0.1 m of a waypoint, because the bearing to a point that close changes quickly with small errors in the estimated position, and the robot would swerve.

Results

With noise off, the run ends at 46.4 s, which includes the 2 s hold in the Done state. The robot ends at (−0.687, 0.973) m, inside i1 and 0.07 m from the center of the box. It enters the box at 39.9 s and stays. The estimate it steers by stayed within 0.015 m and 0.9° of the actual pose, so its decisions rested on good information.

It is slower than the time-based controller, whose robot is in the box after 29.4 s against 39.9 s here. The forward signal drops as the robot nears each waypoint, which probably accounts for most of the difference.

Adding noise

Each controller was run five times with noise on. Before every trial the provided script set the motor noise to 100 and the encoder noise to 50 and drew new random seeds. The actual final position was saved after each trial, and the no-noise runs were added for comparison.

Noise moves the final position of both controllers away from the no-noise result, and the two are affected in different ways. The five time-based finals lie 0.35 to 0.52 m from the no-noise final (mean 0.43 m), and the five sensor-based finals lie 0.01 to 0.51 m away (mean 0.28 m). Three of five trials end with the center of the robot inside i1 for each controller.

The time-based controller has no feedback, so a wheel that runs a little faster or slower than commanded turns directly into a heading and position error that carries into every later segment. Motor noise alone gives the same spread as full noise, and encoder noise alone changes nothing, since that controller never reads the encoders.

With both noise sources on, the sensor-based controller stopped on average 0.05 m from its last waypoint according to its own estimate, while the actual robot was 0.36 m from the estimated position, in the same direction in all five trials. The feedback worked on the information it had, and that information was wrong. I suspect wheel slip under the noisy motors, but I did not test that.

Comparing the two controllers

Without noise, both controllers do the job. The time-based controller is faster (in the box after 29.4 s against 39.9 s) and ends 0.04 m from the center of i1 against 0.07 m. Its timing had to be tuned segment by segment, and it only works for this route, this start and these motors. The sensor-based controller needs only a list of waypoints, and its pose estimate stayed within 0.015 m of the true position.

With noise, the sensor-based finals are less scattered. Over ten noisy runs of each controller, the standard deviation of the final position is 0.09 m in x and 0.15 m in y for the sensor-based controller against 0.21 m and 0.34 m for the time-based one. It does not reach the box more often, though: the center of the robot ends inside i1 in 3 of 10 sensor-based runs and 5 of 10 time-based runs, because the sensor-based finals sit together just to the right of and below the middle of the box, while the time-based finals are spread widely around it.

I would still recommend the sensor-based controller for a real robot. Its error is a consistent offset caused by a wrong pose estimate, which could be reduced by filtering the encoder signals, by modeling the wheels better or by adding a second sensor that corrects the estimate. The time-based controller's error is random and has no feedback to act on, and it would change with the battery, the floor or the load. This recommendation rests on ten runs per controller and a noise model that I could not compare with a real robot, and neither controller would be reliable enough as built.
